A decision console for on-site metal printing · synthetic catalogue, published rules, open code

Which spare parts should a fleet have the right to print?

A fleet of vehicles, vessels or aircraft waits weeks for spare parts that a metal printer could make in days, at the depot or in a container next to the fleet. Most of those parts should still not be printed. This console takes a catalogue of spare parts and answers, part by part, the four questions a sustainment desk asks: can it be printed, is it worth it, what does a deployable printer buy in readiness, and which acceptance tier does a printed part earn. At the default settings parts are worth printing outright, deserve a closer look, should be left alone, and : printing wins on days, not on price.

Before the console · five things to know

Metal 3D printing here means laser powder-bed fusion: a laser melts a 50-micron layer of metal powder, a new layer is spread, and the part grows layer by layer inside a machine the size of a wardrobe. Denmark's industrial 3D-print centre in Aarhus runs several such machines.

A spare part is worth printing when the wait for it is long, the platform it belongs to matters, and the supplier is slow, gone or unwilling. It is rarely worth printing on unit price alone.

The right to print is the licence to make a part from its digital model instead of buying it. Denmark's defence procurement agency has said it now buys that right for some spare parts; the right is worth nothing without the model, the material specification and the load case.

A deployable printer is a metal printer built into a shipping container so parts can be made where the fleet is. It changes lead time from months to days, for the parts that fit it, as long as powder and printer hours last.

A printed part is released into service at an acceptance tier. The Danish-led DISCMAM project uses four: permanent, temporary, emergency, scrap, decided from measured density, geometry and coupon tests. Who may sign which tier is a policy, and this console makes that policy explicit.

Eight terms used below

LPBF laser powder-bed fusion, the metal printing process assumed throughout
DED directed energy deposition: a robot-held laser melts wire or powder onto a surface; larger parts and repairs, rougher finish
build envelope the largest box a machine can print; a part must fit it in some orientation
criticality 1 stops the platform, 2 degrades it, 3 is cosmetic or convenience
lead time days from ordering a part to holding it
obsolescence the supplier status: active, end of production, or gone
witness coupon a small test bar printed next to the part and pulled apart to prove the build's material properties
density how much of the printed volume is solid metal; 99.9 percent is DISCMAM's gate for permanent use, the rest is pores

Act one · Which parts

Sixty-three parts, two scores, one verdict each

Every part gets a technical score (does the process want this part: does it fit a machine, is there a printable alloy, is the shape complex enough to gain, how much finishing will it need) and a business score (does the fleet want it printed: lead time times criticality, supplier status, demand, unit cost). The weights are printed next to each criterion so they can be argued with. Click any dot to read its scorecard, what single change of fact would flip its verdict, and what to ask the supplier before paying for a print right.

What the screening shows. The catalogue is synthetic, the rules are public, and the verdicts still fall the way real screenings fall: a small print list, a long investigate list, and a leave list of cheap, quick or unprintable parts. The value of the exercise is the scorecard, not the dot: every verdict names the fact that carries it, and the flip list says which fact to go and check first.
One part all the way · what printing does to a design

A hinge bracket, optimised for stiffness at 35 percent of the material, with and without the printer's rule

Take one bracket from the catalogue family (a wing-attachment hinge, clamped to a wall on the left, carrying a downward load at the pin on the right) and ask an optimiser to place 35 percent of the material where it stiffens the part most. Unconstrained, it draws the shape a machinist could never make. Then tell it the part will be printed and that nothing may overhang more than 45 degrees over empty space in the build direction, and it redraws itself for each way of standing the part on the plate. Pick a design. Each is computed here, by the classic density method with Langelaar's printability filter, and each is scored for how much of it would need support in every build direction.

What the printer's rule does to the design.

Whether the printed part comes out the shape it was drawn is the other half of the design consequence. Chapter three calibrates a distortion model to NIST's AM-Bench bridge and shows what compensation buys, and what a model error costs.

Act two · Is it worth it

Printing never wins on unit price. Move the sliders and watch what it wins on.

For every printable part: the cost to print one (machine hours, powder, support removal, machining of the functional faces, heat treatment, a CT scan for critical parts, setup) against the conventional price; the days the fleet would wait less; what a day of a stopped platform costs; how often the part is not on the shelf when it fails. Summed over the fleet's remaining life and discounted, minus the one-off cost of qualifying the part family, that is what a print right is worth. If it is worth less than nothing, buy the part.

Printing does not win on price in this catalogue. What printing buys is days, and days are worth money only for parts that stop a platform and are not on the shelf. A console that showed printing winning on price would mislead the desk that has to defend the budget. The last column combines both acts: a part that act one left alone stays left alone however good its arithmetic looks here.
Act three · What a container buys

Readiness over ninety days, with and without a printer next to the fleet

A simulation: every platform in the fleet can lose any of the printable critical parts at the catalogue's failure rate. When a part fails and is not on the shelf, the platform is down until the part arrives: by the conventional route, or printed in the container (one printer, its own powder stock, a queue), or, if the container is busy or out of powder, printed at the home centre and shipped back. Set the fleet, the printers and the powder, then run it. The line is the share of the fleet that is operational each day.

Where one container stops keeping up

The same simulation at the default settings, swept over fleet size. The gap between the container line and the conventional line is what the container buys; where the lines meet, one printer's queue has become the new waiting list.

Context, method, limits

How this was built

The catalogue is generated from nineteen part families with a fixed random seed; the families are shaped on the five DISCMAM use cases named in public (an air compressor cover, a fuel filter housing, a maintenance tool, a front pulley, a joint-shaft flange) and on the items a vehicle, vessel or aircraft sustainment desk sees. No part is a real item from any fleet. Machine envelopes, alloys and the density gate come from published sources; hour rates, powder prices, build rates and qualification costs are assumptions and are marked as such in the table below. The decision model is one JavaScript file that both this page and the build script run, so every number in the prose above was computed by the same code that moves when you move a slider. The bracket is optimised in Python with the classic density method and Langelaar's additive-manufacturing filter, with exact sensitivities through the filter.

Limits

A synthetic catalogue proves the method, not any fleet's answer; the first real use is to load a real parts list. Scores use attributes, not geometry: a real screening reads the CAD for wall thickness, trapped volumes and support access. The cost model is a first-order one with assumed rates; DTI's own parameters are not public and are not used. The bracket is a two-dimensional section under one load case, linear elastic, unqualified. The readiness simulation treats failures as independent and ignores cannibalisation, partial stock and transport risk. The acceptance policy is illustrative except for the one gate taken from DISCMAM. Nothing here is a certification claim.
