The right to print · chapter three · distortion, on a public benchmark

Will the part come out the shape it was drawn?

A metal part printed layer by layer is full of locked-in stress by the time it is finished. Cut it from the plate and it springs. In 2018 the US standards institute printed a small bridge, cut its legs free and measured the spring: at the far end. It then asked the world's modellers to predict that number blind. None of them met the criteria. This chapter does not claim to; it calibrates one scalar to that measurement, and then does the two things a centre can do with such a model: read the shape of the springback along the part and the legs that carry it, and pre-deform the design so the printed part comes out flat, which works exactly in the model and only as well as the model in the machine.

Before the chapter · five things to know

In laser powder-bed fusion each new layer is melted onto a colder part below. As the melt cools it wants to shrink; the part below will not let it. The layer ends up stretched, the part below squeezed, and the print finishes with residual stress locked inside it.

While the part is welded to the build plate the plate holds it straight. Cut it free and the stress is released: the part springs, usually upward at the ends, and a flat design comes out curved.

The AM-Bench bridge is a benchmark part built by NIST: 75 mm long, 12.5 mm tall, 5 mm wide, standing on twelve small legs and one thick end. The legs are cut with a wire and the rise of the eleven ridges on top is measured. The measurement is public; the modelling was a blind competition.

The inherent-strain method is the workhorse model: instead of simulating the laser, each layer is given the shrinkage it is left with once it has cooled and yielded, and the part is built up layer by layer with that shrinkage fighting the structure below. One number, the inherent strain, carries all the thermal detail.

Compensation means printing the mirror image of the predicted springback so that design plus spring equals the drawing. It is only as right as the model that predicted the spring, and that is what the last act measures.

Six terms used below

ridge one of eleven raised strips along the top of the bridge; the measurement points
leg one of twelve thin supports under the bridge, cut free after the build; leg 1 is under ridge 1
inherent strain, e* the locked-in shrinkage per layer, dimensionless; negative means the layer wants to be shorter
springback the movement of the part when the legs are cut, here the rise of each ridge in millimetres
calibration choosing e* so the model reproduces one measured number; the opposite of a blind prediction
pre-deformation the mirror-image design that is meant to come out flat

Act two · The correction

Print the mirror image, and it comes out flat, if the model was right

Compensation is arithmetic once the springback is known: subtract it from the drawing, print that, and the spring puts the ridges back on the line. In a linear model that is exact after one iteration, which tells you nothing. The question that matters is what happens when the model is wrong by a plausible amount, which the 2018 blind round says it will be. Move the model error and read the residual.
